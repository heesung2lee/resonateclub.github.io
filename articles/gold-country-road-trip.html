Gold Country Road Trip: California's 1849 Mining Towns & Gorgeous Backroads

Updated AUGUST 2026 · CalTravel Guide
· By CalTravel Guide Editorial Team

Somewhere between the pine-dotted ridges and the plank-built storefronts of California's western Sierra foothills, the state's past and present seem to breathe in the same breath. This is the Gold Country, the wide slope where the 1849 Gold Rush pulled more than 300,000 hopefuls west — and where the roads still follow the dreams they chased.

Drive it slowly. The point of a Gold Country road trip is not to cover miles, but to let each small town file its own claim on you.

The Golden Chain Highway: Driving the Old West

The region’s signature route is the Golden Chain Highway, a twirling ribbon of road that strings the historic towns together. Old wooden buildings lean into shaded lanes, and when the pavement splinters into quiet side roads you can pull over and picnic along the hillsides and pine groves that watch over the route. The drive itself becomes a kind of time travel.

  • Golden Chain Highway — the scenic spine of the Gold Country, threading plank-era towns and forest.
  • Picnic & hiking stops — grassy slopes and oak groves made for a slow midday break.
  • Weekday pace — backroads stay quiet and shareable, while weekends convene around the bigger towns.

Columbia, Sonora & Placerville: Living History

The best way to step into 1849 is Columbia State Historic Park, a working outdoor museum that preserves a gold-rush streetscape of clapboard stores and handcrafted shops. Kids and the young at heart can try gold panning and listen for the creak of wagon wheels. From there, Sonora and Placerville extend the spell with old bookstores, corner cafés and the kind of awnings you expect a prospector to lean against. Families and solo travelers alike feel the era’s voice here without a shred of fuss.

Apple Hill & the Wine Country

Come autumn, the hills of Apple Hill in El Dorado County fill with the smell of fresh-pressed cider, and the lesser-known wineries of Amador and Placer counties pour deep, characterful wines from old-vine grapes. Small craft breweries dot the roadside too, so no matter how you lean, there’s a tasting room for you. On a warm evening, sip a local glass on an outdoor terrace and let the light over the foothills do the rest.

Sacramento: The Farm-to-Fork Finish

Cap the loop in the state capital. Sacramento runs on farm-to-fork cooking, drawing on the farms and ranches that surround it. Stroll Capitol Park, then sit down to a meal built around what the region grows that very week. Galleries and cheerful cafés keep the city humming, and the historic port district — Old Sacramento — lines the riverbank with shops and eateries.

This is land that first drew people chasing fortune and now rewards anyone who slows down enough to listen.

Tip: Historic towns are busiest on weekends. Arrive early to wander the quiet, photo-worthy streets before the crowds — and pack layers, since foothill evenings cool off fast.
