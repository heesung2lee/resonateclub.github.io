7 golf etiquette and culture differences in California

Updated October 2026 · GoldenPath Guide
· By GoldenPath Guide Editorial Team

American golf culture runs on different defaults than golf in Korea or Japan, and California adds its own relaxed layer on top. Rounds are faster, dress codes are looser, carts are everywhere, and strangers share fairways as a matter of routine. None of this is difficult, but each difference catches first-time visitors once. Here are the seven that matter most.

The short version: keep pace, tip the service staff, and talk to people. American courses forgive a bad swing instantly and a slow round never. Everything below follows from that one idea.

1. Pace of play is the real rulebook

American courses expect a foursome to finish 18 holes in about 4 to 4.5 hours, and marshals enforce it. A ranger driving up to your group is not a suggestion; it means the group behind has waited twice and the course is now managing you. Ready golf is the norm: whoever is ready hits, regardless of whose ball is farthest, except on the greens where order still mostly holds.

Ball searches get about 3 minutes under the modern rules, not the old 5, and most California courses ask you to wave faster groups through at par 3s if a gap opens. Lost-ball holes go quicker if everyone watches every tee shot. Call out landing spots as a habit and your group saves 20 minutes a round.

2. Pairing with strangers is normal

Book as a single or a pair and the pro shop will place you with other golfers to fill a foursome. This is standard practice, not a demotion, and refusing a pairing marks you as difficult. Introduce yourself on the first tee with a name and a handshake, state your rough handicap, and mention which tees you plan to play.

Small talk carries the round. Americans discuss the course, the weather, and where everyone is from, usually in that order. You do not need fluent English; short answers plus interest in the other person cover it. I have played some of my best California rounds in groups where nobody shared a first language.

Culture tip: On the first tee, say your name, where you are visiting from, and that you are happy to play ready golf. Those three sentences answer every question an American pairing partner has about you.

3. Carts have rules you must read

California courses are built for carts, and the daily rules change with turf conditions. Cart path only means all four wheels stay on the path, no exceptions. The 90-degree rule means drive on the path, turn out at your ball, and return straight back. Desert courses in Palm Springs often restrict carts to paths in overseed season, roughly October through November.

Park carts behind greens and beside tees, never in front, and keep them off tee boxes and green surrounds entirely. The cart girl or beverage cart that circles the course sells drinks and snacks for cash or card; tipping a dollar or two per purchase is customary. Return the cart with trash removed and the sand bottle refilled if the course provides one.

4. Tipping follows service, not the green fee

You do not tip on the green fee itself, but you tip nearly everyone who touches your bag. Bag-drop staff who load clubs onto carts commonly receive 3 to 5 dollars per bag. Forecaddies, who guide groups on foot without carrying bags, generally receive 20 to 30 dollars per player for the round. Full caddies at places like Pebble Beach command higher rates, published on the official caddie page.

Locker-room attendants, shuttle drivers, and practice-range staff fall in the same 2 to 5 dollar range. Carry small bills from day one, because the bag drop comes before any ATM. When service is genuinely bad, Americans reduce the tip rather than skipping the conversation; a quiet word with the pro shop fixes more than silence.

5. Dress codes are simpler, with three traps

Collared shirts and golf shorts or slacks cover nearly every California course, and many allow modern athletic golf wear. Denim is the most common refusal: jeans of any color get turned away at resort and upscale daily-fee courses. Swimwear, gym shorts, and sleeveless shirts for men fail at the same doors. Metal spikes have been banned almost everywhere since the early 2000s, so wear soft spikes or spikeless shoes.

Hats come off inside clubhouses and restaurants at traditional clubs, a habit worth keeping everywhere. Women should check each course page, since definitions of acceptable length and collar vary slightly by venue. When uncertain, the pro shop answers dress questions kindly if you call before the round instead of arguing at the door.

6. Walking versus riding is a course decision

Many California resort courses require carts or caddies and simply do not allow walking, especially in desert heat. Links-style and municipal courses like Torrey Pines welcome walkers but involve real hills; the South course climbs enough that tired legs cost strokes after the 13th. Push carts rent for a small fee at most walking-friendly courses.

Caddie culture peaks at Pebble Beach, where walking with a caddie is the classic experience and carts are restricted. Book caddies at least two weeks ahead in season, confirm the meeting point, and walk the course the evening before if the trails allow it. Your caddie reads greens, rakes bunkers you visit, and quietly keeps the group on pace.

7. Phones, music, and talking to staff

Phone calls on the course annoy American golfers more than almost anything else. Keep the phone silent, step far from tees and greens for urgent calls, and never hold a conversation while someone in your group is over a shot. Music from portable speakers divides opinion sharply: confirm with the whole group before playing a single track, and keep volume low enough that the next fairway hears nothing.

Talk to the staff more than feels natural. Starters, rangers, and pro-shop staff hold the local knowledge that saves strokes: which holes play into the wind, where the hidden hazards sit, what the greens did this morning. A two-minute question at the starter hut beats two hours of discovering it yourself. Thank the group behind you if they waited, and thank the staff on the way out; Americans remember courtesy longer than scores.

A one-minute pre-round checklist

  1. Confirm cart rules, dress code, and phone policy on the course page the night before.
  2. Carry small bills for bag drop, beverage cart, and locker room.
  3. Introduce yourself to pairing partners with name, home, and tee choice.
  4. Watch every tee shot in your group and call the landing spot.
  5. Keep the phone silent and music off unless the whole group agrees.

Do these five and every California course treats you like a regular by the turn. The golf takes care of itself after that.
