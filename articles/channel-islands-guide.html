Channel Islands National Park Guide: Santa Cruz, Anacapa & Marine Life

Updated AUGUST 2026 · CalTravel Guide
· By CalTravel Guide Editorial Team

Just off the Southern California coast, a chain of islands feels a world removed from the freeways and surf towns of the mainland. Channel Islands National Park is often called the “Galapagos of North America” for a simple reason: its five islands — Anacapa, Santa Cruz, Santa Rosa, San Miguel and Santa Barbara — are home to species found nowhere else, with no permanent human population and a coastline of rugged cliffs and hidden sea caves. Because two miles of ocean sit between here and the mainland, an island day trip is a genuinely wild escape.

Getting There

There is no bridge and no easy road. The islands are reached only by boat or plane, primarily via Island Packers ferries from Ventura or Oxnard, or Channel Islands Aviation flights from Camarillo. Book your passage well ahead, especially in summer. The crossing itself is part of the experience — watch for dolphins riding the bow wave.

Anacapa

Tiny Anacapa is the closest island and the easiest half-day trip. Its signature is Arch Rock, a sea arch rising from the water, and a picturesque lighthouse perched on the cliffs. A short, stepped trail leads to the lighthouse viewpoint, and the surrounding waters are a protected marine sanctuary.

Santa Cruz

The largest island at around 96 square miles, Santa Cruz is the island to visit for real adventure. It offers long hiking trails, secluded beaches and the famous Painted Cave — one of the largest sea caves in the world, best explored by kayak. Rent a kayak in Scorpion Anchorage, or join a guided paddle to drift inside the cave’s glow. Santa Cruz also has the most visitor infrastructure, making it the best choice for a first trip or a family day out, with rangers on hand and a few trails graded for all ages.

San Miguel

Remote San Miguel is the place for wildlife. Its beaches host huge rookeries of seals and sea lions, and the island is reachable only with a ranger permit. If you want to see pinnipeds sunning by the hundreds, this is the island to target.

Marine Life

The cold, nutrient-rich waters around the islands are alive. Year-round you can spot whales, dolphins and seals; blue whales pass through in summer, gray whales migrate in winter, and pinnipeds — seals and sea lions — are everywhere. Kayaking, hiking, camping and diving all operate around the islands, and the diving is considered some of the best on the West Coast.

Stargazing & Where to Camp

With no city lights for miles, the islands are a stargazer’s dream — remote, dark and quiet. Camping is allowed on several islands, letting you stay for a sunset, a moonless night and a true dark-sky sunrise.

Best Time to Visit

Seas are calmest and the crossing smoothest in summer (June–August), which is also the busiest season with the warmest weather. Spring and fall bring mild temperatures, wildflower blooms in spring and fewer crowds. Book the ferry early in any season, and pack layers — island weather changes quickly.

Day Trip vs Overnight

A ferry day trip to Anacapa or Santa Cruz is the easiest introduction — board in the morning, spend a few hours on a trail, and be back on the mainland by evening. If your budget and schedule allow, an overnight stay is far more rewarding: the island empties out when the last ferry leaves, and you get the sea-cliff sunset, a truly dark night sky and the calm of sunrise all to yourself. Tents and camping gear are the only way to stay, so pack accordingly.

Plan ahead: Because the islands are only reachable by boat or plane, ferry and flight seats are limited. Book your crossing in advance, confirm your return time, and bring water, sunscreen and food — there are no stores and no permanent residents out here.
